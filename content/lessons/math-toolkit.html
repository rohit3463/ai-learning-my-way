Why start here

Every model in this course is built from a handful of ideas: vectors, distances, matrices, slopes and chances. None of them needs more than school maths. This lesson collects them in one place, so later lessons can use them without stopping to explain.

Skim what you already know. Each section says where it comes back later.

Vectors

A vector is an ordered list of numbers:

\[ a = (3, 2) \]

With two numbers you can draw it as an arrow: 3 across and 2 up (Act I). With more numbers you can't draw it, but everything below still works the same way. In machine learning a vector usually describes one example: a house as (size, rooms, age), or a message as a count of each word.

Adding and scaling

Add two vectors by adding matching entries. On paper, put one arrow at the tip of the other:

\[ (3, 2) + (1, 2) = (3 + 1,\ 2 + 2) = (4, 4) \]

Multiply a vector by a number (a scalar) by multiplying every entry. It stretches, shrinks, or with a negative number flips the arrow:

\[ 2 \times (3, 2) = (6, 4) \qquad -1 \times (3, 2) = (-3, -2) \]

Length

The length of a vector comes from Pythagoras: square each entry, add, take the square root.

\[ |a| = \sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2 + \dots + a_n^2} \qquad |(3, 2)| = \sqrt{9 + 4} = 3.61 \]

Distance

The distance between two points \(p\) and \(q\) is the length of the arrow from one to the other, \(q - p\):

\[ d(p, q) = \sqrt{(q_1 - p_1)^2 + (q_2 - p_2)^2 + \dots} \]

From \((-3, -1)\) to \((2, 2)\): the arrow is \((5, 3)\), so the distance is \(\sqrt{25 + 9} = 5.83\) (Act II).

Comes back in: k-nearest neighbours (which points are close?), SVM (how wide is the street?), and anywhere we ask how different two examples are.

The dot product

Multiply matching entries of two vectors and add the results. Two vectors go in, one number comes out:

\[ u \cdot v = u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2 + \dots + u_n v_n \qquad (3, 1) \cdot (2, 4) = 6 + 4 = 10 \]

It also measures how much two arrows point the same way:

\[ u \cdot v = |u| \times |v| \times \cos(\text{angle between them}) \]
  • positive: they point roughly the same way
  • zero: they are at right angles
  • negative: they point roughly apart

Dividing by both lengths leaves just the cosine, called the cosine similarity. It is 1 for the same direction and ignores how long the arrows are. It is a common way to compare tastes, documents or images (Act III).

Comes back in: every model's score. \(w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b\) is the dot product \(w \cdot x\) plus \(b\).

Matrices

A matrix is a grid of numbers with rows and columns. A matrix with 2 rows and 3 columns is called "2 by 3".

\[ M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \]

Matrix times vector

Each row of the matrix takes a dot product with the vector:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a x + b y \\ c x + d y \end{pmatrix} \]

Seen as a picture, a matrix moves vectors. It can turn, stretch or slant the whole plane. Its first column is where the arrow \((1, 0)\) lands, and its second column is where \((0, 1)\) lands (Act IV). For example, \(\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) turns everything a quarter turn to the left.

Matrix times matrix

Entry (row \(i\), column \(j\)) of \(A \times B\) is the dot product of row \(i\) of \(A\) with column \(j\) of \(B\). The result does both moves in one: first \(B\), then \(A\). Order matters: \(A \times B\) is usually not the same as \(B \times A\).

\[ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \cdot 5 + 2 \cdot 7 & 1 \cdot 6 + 2 \cdot 8 \\ 3 \cdot 5 + 4 \cdot 7 & 3 \cdot 6 + 4 \cdot 8 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 19 & 22 \\ 43 & 50 \end{pmatrix} \]

The transpose \(M^{T}\) swaps rows and columns. The identity matrix (1s on the diagonal, 0s elsewhere) changes nothing.

Comes back in: a whole dataset is a matrix (one row per example), and the weights of a neural network are matrices.

Derivatives

The derivative of a function \(f(x)\), written \(f'(x)\) or \(\dfrac{df}{dx}\), is its slope at a point: how much \(f\) changes for a tiny change in \(x\). Take two points close together and measure the rise over the run, then bring them closer (Act V):

\[ f'(x) \approx \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} \quad \text{for a very small } h \]

The chain rule

For a function inside a function, multiply the outer slope by the inner slope:

\[ \frac{d}{dx} f(g(x)) = f'(g(x)) \times g'(x) \]

This is exactly how Lesson 1 gets its gradient. With \(e = y - w x - b\), the squared error \(e^2\) has slope \(2e\) with respect to \(e\), and \(e\) has slope \(-x\) with respect to \(w\), so:

\[ \frac{d}{dw} (y - w x - b)^2 = 2(y - w x - b) \times (-x) \]

Several inputs: the gradient

When a function has several inputs, such as a loss \(L(w, b)\), take the slope with respect to one input while holding the others still. That is a partial derivative, written \(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w}\). The list of all of them is the gradient. It points uphill, and stepping the other way is gradient descent:

\[ w \leftarrow w - \eta \times \frac{\partial L}{\partial w} \]

Comes back in: every model trained by gradient descent: linear and logistic regression, SVMs and neural networks.

Exponentials and logarithms

\(e = 2.718\ldots\) is a special number, and \(e^x\) grows faster and faster. The natural logarithm \(\ln x\) undoes it: \(\ln(e^x) = x\). Two log rules turn multiplication into addition:

\[ \ln(a \times b) = \ln a + \ln b \qquad \ln(a^n) = n \ln a \]

Comes back in: the sigmoid \(1 \div (1 + e^{-z})\) and the log loss in logistic regression, and adding log-chances in naive Bayes.

Probability

A probability is a number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). When all outcomes are equally likely:

\[ P(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{outcomes where it happens}}{\text{all outcomes}} \]

Two dice have 36 equally likely outcomes, and 6 of them sum to 7, so \(P(\text{sum} = 7) = 6 \div 36 = 0.167\). Roll many times and the share of sevens settles toward that value (Act VI).

The rules you will need

RuleFormula
not A\(P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)\)
A and B, if independent\(P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B)\)
A given B\(P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{P(A \text{ and } B)}{P(B)}\)
Bayes' rule\(P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{P(B \mid A) \times P(A)}{P(B)}\)

"Given" means "now that we know B happened". For example, the chance of a total above 9 is \(6 \div 36 = 0.17\), but given that the first die shows 6 it jumps to \(3 \div 6 = 0.5\).

Averages

The mean (average) of \(n\) numbers is their sum divided by \(n\). Most losses in this course are a mean: the average squared error, the average log loss, the average hinge cost.

\[ \text{mean} = \frac{x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n}{n} \]

Books often write a sum with the Greek letter sigma: \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i\) means \(x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n\). This course writes sums out in full, but now you can read both.

Comes back in: logistic regression (probabilities), naive Bayes (conditional probability and Bayes' rule), and every loss (means).
